Nothing about what a buyer wants changed. They still want a shop that can hit the
in-hands date, run the size they need, and not surprise them on the invoice. What
changed is the first place they go to find one, and how much of the deciding happens
before they talk to anybody.

## Where does the search actually start now?

For a growing share of buyers it starts inside an assistant — ChatGPT, Perplexity, or
the AI summary sitting above Google's results — rather than in a list of blue links.

Google began showing AI Overviews to all US users in May 2024, which means the default
search experience for an American buyer sourcing a supplier now leads with a written
answer, not a set of options. A buyer typing "contract embroidery near me with a low
minimum" gets a paragraph naming shops before they get a single result to click.

The rep relationship has not gone away. It is just no longer the only door, and it is
not the door a new buyer uses.

## Why do fewer buyers click through to a shop's website?

Because the answer already told them what they wanted to know.

If the summary states that a shop runs DTF, takes 24-piece minimums and turns work in
five business days, the buyer has what they needed to shortlist. The click that used to
land on your capabilities page now doesn't happen. Your site still did the work — it is
where the assistant read those facts — but the visit shows up in nobody's analytics.

This is the part that catches shop owners out. Traffic can be flat or falling while
your name is appearing in more answers than ever, and the first signal you get is a
call from someone who says "ChatGPT sent me."

## What does an AI assistant read when it picks which shops to name?

It reads text that plainly states what you do, and it prefers text it does not have to
interpret.

That is an unglamorous list: a page that says which decoration methods you run, a
minimum stated as a number, a turnaround stated in business days, a service area named
as places rather than "the surrounding area." Structured data — the machine-readable
markup describing your business — removes the remaining guesswork.

What it does not read well is a gallery of job photos, a homepage carousel, or a PDF
line sheet. Those are the places a lot of shops keep their most useful information.

The research points the same way. The KDD 2024 paper that coined the term *generative
engine optimization* tested nine content strategies and found the largest gains came
from adding quotations from authoritative sources and adding relevant statistics —
in other words, from being specific and citing things, not from keyword density.

## What does this change for a decorator?

The buyer's shortlist is now assembled by something that has never seen your work.

A press with good registration, a sample that came back clean, a rep who remembered a
customer's PMS from last year — none of that reaches the model. It only knows what is
written down somewhere it can read. Shops that have been coasting on reputation and
referral are the most exposed, because reputation is exactly the asset that does not
travel into an answer.

The flip side is that this is winnable by a small shop. A four-person embroiderer that
states its minimums clearly will get named ahead of a twenty-head operation whose site
says "quality decoration solutions since 1994."

## What should a shop do first?

Ask the assistants the question your buyers ask, and read the answer.

Open ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google, and type the sourcing question a real customer
would use — your city, your decoration method, a realistic run size and minimum. Write
down which shops get named. If you are not in the answer, you now know precisely what
you are competing against, because the answer names them.
