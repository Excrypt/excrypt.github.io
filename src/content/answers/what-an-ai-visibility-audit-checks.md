An audit is not a report full of scores. It is four checks, and each one produces
something you can act on or hand to somebody else.

## What does the audit ask the assistants?

It asks the sourcing questions your buyers actually ask, and records who gets named.

We write ten to fifteen questions in the language a real buyer uses — a city, a
decoration method, a run size, a minimum, a deadline. "Who does contract embroidery near
Dallas with a 48-piece minimum." "Screen printer in Columbus that can run 500 tees by
Friday." Then we put each one to ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google's AI Overview and Gemini,
and save the answers.

You get the transcripts. Not a summary of them — the actual answers, with the shops
that got named and the pages the assistant drew on. In most audits the client is in
none of them, and the same two or three competitors are in most of them.

## What does it check on your own site?

Whether the facts a buyer needs are stated in words, on a page, in one place.

The checklist is short and dull, which is the point:

- Decoration methods named individually — screen print, embroidery, DTF, DTG, heat
  transfer, sublimation — rather than grouped as "custom apparel."
- Minimums stated as numbers, per method, because they usually differ.
- Turnaround stated in business days, and whether you take rush work.
- Run sizes you will and will not quote.
- Whether you take contract work for other shops and distributors, which is a
  different question from whether you sell retail.
- Service area written as named places.

Most shops have every one of these facts in their head and none of them on a page. The
information tends to live in a gallery, a quote form, or a PDF — three formats an
assistant reads badly or not at all.

## What structured data does it look for?

Whether your site tells a machine what it is, instead of leaving it to be inferred.

Structured data is markup that states plainly what a page describes: that this is a
business, that its address is this, that it offers these services in this area. Google's
own documentation describes it as making page content explicit rather than leaving it to
be worked out from the text. The audit checks whether you have `LocalBusiness` markup,
whether the address and phone in it match the ones a customer would call, and whether
any of it contradicts what is on the visible page.

A surprising amount of the time there is markup, and it is wrong — left behind by a
theme, naming a business that moved four years ago.

## Why does consistency across listings matter?

Because a contradiction gives an assistant a reason to name somebody else.

We compare your name, address, phone, hours and described services across your site,
your Google Business Profile, and the industry directories — SAGE, ASI, and the
regional listings. Two different phone numbers, an old suite number, or hours that
disagree with the ones on your door are all common, and all resolvable in an afternoon.

This is the least interesting part of the audit and often the one that moves first.

## What do you get at the end?

A document, a call, and a list in priority order.

The document has the assistant transcripts, the gaps found on your site, the structured
data that is missing or wrong, and every listing discrepancy with the correct value
beside it. The call is about half an hour and walks through it. The list at the back is
ordered by what will change an answer soonest, and it is written so that you could hand
it to whoever built your site and have them do the work without us.

Most people don't. But you could, and we would rather say that plainly than pretend the
list is a secret.
